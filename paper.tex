\begin{abstract}
For \(a>0\), we prove positivity of the solution of the quartic discrete
Painlev\'e-type recurrence arising from the quantum minimal surface
\(w_2=w_1^3\).  This proves the \(d=4\), \(a>0\) case of
Felder--Hoppe Conjecture~3.6 \cite{FelderHoppe2025}.  The initial data
are given by a quadratic combination
of Bessel functions of orders \(\pm\frac14\).  The proof converts the Bessel
amplitude into a positive Laplace integral and uses two quartic source
variables to reduce the four residue classes modulo \(4\) to one family
of bi-moment matrices.  A triangular factorization and Andr\'eief's
identity fix the strict sign
of every leading principal minor in the four symmetry blocks and give
an explicit positive
multiple-integral formula for each minor once its predicted sign is
divided out.  Consequently all signed
orthogonal-polynomial norms are positive, and their scaled consecutive
ratios give \(v_n(a)>0\) for every \(a>0\) and \(n\geq0\).
No claim is made about an oscillatory or regularized interpretation at
the endpoint \(a=0\).
\vskip5pt
\noindent\textbf{Keywords.}\enspace
discrete Painlev\'e-type recurrence, indefinite orthogonal polynomials,
quartic potential, bi-moment determinant, quantum minimal surface.
\vskip5pt
\noindent\textbf{MSC2020 Classification.}\enspace
Primary 34M55; Secondary 39A36, 33C45, 15B52, 53A10.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Discrete Painlev\'e-type recurrences occur in matrix regularizations of
minimal surfaces and in the recurrence coefficients of orthogonal
polynomials.  To our knowledge, the operator formulation of quantum
minimal surfaces has its origins in \cite{CornalbaTaylor1998} and was
developed further in \cite{ArnlindHoppeKontsevich2019}, where higher
recurrences are discussed.  The quartic recurrence below is written explicitly in
\cite[equation~(38)]{Hoppe2025} and in \cite{ClarksonEtAl2025}; those
works also treat the
special-function positive solution in the \(d=3\), or cubic-potential,
case.

For the quantum minimal surface associated with the holomorphic curve
\(w_2=w_1^{d-1}\), Felder and Hoppe
\cite{FelderHoppe2025} constructed an indefinite Hermitian form and
showed that, whenever the relevant leading Gram matrices are
nondegenerate, its orthogonal-polynomial norm ratios obey
\begin{equation}
V_n+\frac1{a^2}\sum_{j=0}^{d-2}
\prod_{k=0}^{d-2}V_{n+j-k}=\frac{n+1}{a}.
\label{eq:FH-recurrence}
\end{equation}

For \(d=4\), after setting \(v_n=V_n/a\) and
\(\epsilon=a^{-2}\), the recurrence becomes
\begin{equation}
v_n\left(
1+v_{n-1}v_{n-2}
+v_{n+1}v_{n-1}
+v_{n+2}v_{n+1}
\right)
=(n+1)\epsilon ,
\qquad n\geq0,
\label{eq:quartic-recurrence}
\end{equation}
where \(v_n=0\) for \(n<0\).  The two initial values are determined by
\begin{equation}
v_0=-\frac1a\frac{h'(a)}{h(a)},
\qquad
v_1=-\frac1a\frac{h''(a)}{h'(a)},
\label{eq:initial-values}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
h(a)=\frac{\pi^2a}{4}\bigg[
&J_{-1/4}\left(\frac{a^2}{4}\right)^2
-\sqrt2\,J_{-1/4}\left(\frac{a^2}{4}\right)
J_{1/4}\left(\frac{a^2}{4}\right)\\
&+J_{1/4}\left(\frac{a^2}{4}\right)^2
\bigg].
\end{split}
\label{eq:h-bessel}
\end{equation}
Felder and Hoppe proved the required Gram nondegeneracy for
\(d=1,2,3\) and formulated it as a conjecture for general \(d\)
\cite[Conjecture~3.6 and Theorem~3.7]{FelderHoppe2025}.
The quartic case is the first one not covered by their argument.  Their
conjecture is phrased for \(a\geq0\).  At \(a=0\), however, the planar
moments are no longer absolutely convergent and an oscillatory or
regularized interpretation must be specified.  We work throughout in
the canonical absolutely convergent range \(a>0\), where the result
below proves the \(a>0\) part of the \(d=4\) assertion.  We make no claim
about an endpoint interpretation at \(a=0\).

Our result is the following.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For every \(a>0\), the initial values
\eqref{eq:initial-values}--\eqref{eq:h-bessel} extend uniquely by
\eqref{eq:quartic-recurrence} to a sequence defined for every
\(n\geq0\).  This sequence satisfies
\[
v_n(a)>0\qquad\text{for every }n\geq0.
\]
In particular, the \(d=4\), \(a>0\) case of Felder--Hoppe
Conjecture~3.6 \cite{FelderHoppe2025} holds.
\end{theorem}
Here and throughout, an extension means a sequence of finite real
numbers satisfying \eqref{eq:quartic-recurrence} at every index.
Thus the uniqueness assertion concerns the prescribed Bessel initial
data; it is not a classification of positive sequences arising from
other initial data.

The main point is an integral sign mechanism that applies uniformly to
all indices.  The Bessel expression \eqref{eq:h-bessel} has a positive
Laplace representation.  Adding independent source variables for
\(z^4\) and \(\bar z^4\) produces four positive integral kernels, one
for each residue class modulo \(4\).  Their mixed derivative matrices
are reduced by triangular row operations to
\[
\left[
\int_0^\infty s^p
\left(\frac{s^2}{1+s^2}\right)^q\dd\mu(s)
\right]_{p,q=0}^{k-1}.
\]
Both \(s\) and \(s^2/(1+s^2)\) are strictly increasing.  Andr\'eief's
identity therefore expresses the determinant as an integral of a
product of two Vandermonde determinants with a fixed positive sign.
This proves the required sign pattern for every finite block at once.
The monotone-Vandermonde determinant argument belongs to the broader
theory of sign regularity and total positivity; for the terminology and
general framework, see \cite[Chapter~2]{Karlin1968}.  Related
nonsymmetric moment determinants occur in biorthogonal ensembles
\cite{Borodin1998}.  Those standard mechanisms do not by themselves
supply a positive measure for the oscillatory quartic Gram blocks.
Indeed, a single radial source records only the total quartic degree,
whereas the block entry indexed by \((p,q)\) requires the powers of
\(z^4\) and \(\bar z^4\) to be varied independently.  To our knowledge,
the key new ingredient is the two-source identity in
Section~\ref{sec:blocks}.  It first
separates those two powers on truncated disks and then, after the
sources are set equal, converts each of the four blocks into the
positive bi-moment family of Section~\ref{sec:sign-lemma}.  Thus the
standard determinant argument applies only after the new source
representation has removed the oscillatory obstruction.  The closure
into four positive kernels uses the special \(K_{1/2}\) evaluation of
the quartic Bessel amplitude; consequently the same construction does
not automatically extend to higher \(d\).  Keeping track of the
triangular factors also yields an explicit positive multiple-integral
formula for every leading block determinant.

The paper is organized as follows.  Section~\ref{sec:gram} recalls the
quartic Hermitian form and its relation to the recurrence.
Section~\ref{sec:sign-lemma} proves the leading-determinant sign lemma.
Section~\ref{sec:blocks} derives the four positive kernels and applies
the lemma to the four symmetry blocks.  Section~\ref{sec:completion}
then identifies the positive norm ratios with the solution of
\eqref{eq:quartic-recurrence}.

\section{The quartic Gram form}\label{sec:gram}

Fix \(a>0\) and write
\begin{equation}
\rho_a(z)=
\exp\left(-a|z|^2+\frac{\ii}{4}(z^4+\bar z^4)\right).
\label{eq:density}
\end{equation}
The Gaussian factor makes all polynomial moments absolutely
convergent.  Let \(\eta=\e^{\pi\ii/4}\).  Decompose the polynomial ring
into \(C_4\)-weight spaces
\[
\C[z]=\bigoplus_{r=0}^3\mathcal P_r,
\qquad
\mathcal P_r=\{f\in\C[z]:f(\ii z)=\ii^r f(z)\}.
\]
For \(f\in\mathcal P_r\), set
\[
(\sigma f)(z)=(-\eta)^{-r}f(\eta z),
\]
and extend \(\sigma\) linearly to \(\C[z]\).  With
\(\dd A=\dd(\operatorname{Re}z)\dd(\operatorname{Im}z)\), define
\begin{equation}
(f,g)_a
=\int_{\C}f(z)\overline{\sigma g(z)}\rho_a(z)\dd A(z).
\label{eq:pairing}
\end{equation}
We use the convention that \((\ ,\ )_a\) is linear in its first
argument and conjugate-linear in its second.  This form is Hermitian.
Indeed, distinct weight spaces are orthogonal
because the density is invariant under \(z\mapsto\ii z\).  For
monomials in a common weight space, put
\[
M_{pq}^{(r)}
=\int_{\C}z^{r+4p}\bar z^{r+4q}\rho_a(z)\dd A(z).
\]
Since \(\rho_a(\eta z)=\overline{\rho_a(z)}\), the change of variables
\(z\mapsto\eta z\) gives
\[
\overline{M_{qp}^{(r)}}=(-1)^{q-p}M_{pq}^{(r)}.
\]
Together with the twist signs below, this shows
\((z^{r+4p},z^{r+4q})_a
=\overline{(z^{r+4q},z^{r+4p})_a}\), and hence proves the assertion by
sesquilinearity.  If \(m=r+4q\), then
\begin{equation}
\sigma z^m=(-\eta)^{-r}\eta^m z^m=(-1)^{r+q}z^m.
\label{eq:twist-sign}
\end{equation}
Thus the Gram matrix on \(\mathcal P_r\), in the ordered monomial basis
\(z^r,z^{r+4},\ldots\), has entries
\begin{equation}
G_{pq}^{(r)}
=(-1)^{r+q}\int_{\C}
z^{r+4p}\bar z^{r+4q}\rho_a(z)\dd A(z),
\qquad p,q\geq0.
\label{eq:gram-block}
\end{equation}

Assume temporarily that the leading principal minors in all four
blocks are nonzero.  Since distinct weight spaces are orthogonal,
grouping the monomials \(1,z,\ldots,z^{n-1}\) by residue modulo \(4\)
makes their Gram matrix block diagonal, with blocks that are leading
principal submatrices of the four matrices \eqref{eq:gram-block}; the
assumption therefore makes every such Gram matrix nondegenerate, and
Gram--Schmidt gives monic polynomials \(P_n(z)=z^n+\cdots\).
Moreover, orthogonalizing \(z^n\) against \(1,\ldots,z^{n-1}\)
subtracts components only from the weight space of \(z^n\), so
\(P_n\in\mathcal P_{n\bmod4}\).  For \(\ell\in\mathbb Z\), define
\begin{equation}
\tau(\ell)=
\begin{cases}
-1,&\ell\equiv3\pmod4,\\
1,&\ell\not\equiv3\pmod4,
\end{cases}
\qquad
\varrho(n)=(-1)^n\prod_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\tau(\ell)
=(-1)^{n+\lfloor n/4\rfloor}.
\label{eq:rho-sign}
\end{equation}
The signed norms \(\fh_n\) are fixed by
\begin{equation}
(P_n,P_n)_a=\varrho(n)\fh_n.
\label{eq:signed-norm}
\end{equation}
Thus the scalar function \(h(a)\) in \eqref{eq:h-bessel} will become
the first signed norm \(\fh_0\).
Section~\ref{sec:blocks} will prove simultaneously that all the
required minors are nonzero and that \(\fh_n>0\).

The pairing \eqref{eq:pairing} is precisely the monomial-potential
Hermitian product of Felder and Hoppe for \(d=4\).  Indeed,
\(\eta=\e^{\pi\ii/4}\) satisfies \(\eta^4=-1\); the density
\eqref{eq:density} is the density of
\cite[Section~3.1]{FelderHoppe2025}; the twist \(\sigma\) is the map
of \cite[Proposition~3.1]{FelderHoppe2025} for the right inverse
\(\chi\) chosen there, which is given by \(\chi(r)=r\) for
\(0\leq r\leq3\); the sign \(\tau\) in \eqref{eq:rho-sign} is the
twist sign of \cite[Lemma~3.4]{FelderHoppe2025}; and
\eqref{eq:signed-norm} is their normalization of the signed norms,
which they denote \(h_n\).
Consequently the Gram matrices appearing in
\cite[Conjecture~3.6]{FelderHoppe2025} are exactly the matrices
\(\big[(z^p,z^q)_a\big]_{p,q=0}^{N}\) considered below.

\section{A leading-determinant sign lemma}\label{sec:sign-lemma}

We isolate the integral argument that drives the proof.  Let
\(y>0\), \(\alpha\geq0\), and \(\beta\geq0\), and define
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(w;y)
=\int_0^\infty
\e^{-ys}s^{\alpha-1/2}(1+ws^2)^{-\beta-1/2}\dd s,
\qquad w>0.
\label{eq:Phi}
\end{equation}
For \(p,q\geq0\), put
\begin{equation}
D_{pq}^{\alpha,\beta}
=\left.
\partial_u^p\partial_v^q
\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(uv;y)
\right|_{u=v=1}.
\label{eq:D-def}
\end{equation}
Both \(\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}\) and \(D_{pq}^{\alpha,\beta}\) depend on
the parameter \(y\), which is fixed throughout; the dependence is
suppressed from the notation.

\begin{lemma}[signs of the leading source determinants]
\label{lem:leading-source-signs}
For every \(k\geq1\),
\begin{equation}
\sgn\det
\left[D_{pq}^{\alpha,\beta}\right]_{p,q=0}^{k-1}
=(-1)^{k(k-1)/2}.
\label{eq:D-sign}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Set
\[
b=\beta+\frac12,
\qquad
\gamma=\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac14,
\qquad
L(y)=\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(1;y).
\]
The substitution \(t=\sqrt w\,s\) in \eqref{eq:Phi} gives
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(w;y)
=w^{-\gamma}L\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt w}\right).
\label{eq:Phi-scaling}
\end{equation}
Let \(\vartheta=w\partial_w\), and use the falling-factorial notation
\[
(\vartheta)\fall{p}
=\vartheta(\vartheta-1)\cdots(\vartheta-p+1).
\]
Under \eqref{eq:Phi-scaling}, \(\vartheta\) acts on \(L\) as
\[
\mathcal A=-\gamma-\frac y2\partial_y.
\]
More precisely, for every smooth function \(g\) on \((0,\infty)\), a
direct computation gives
\[
\vartheta\left[w^{-\gamma}g\left(yw^{-1/2}\right)\right]
=w^{-\gamma}(\mathcal Ag)\left(yw^{-1/2}\right),
\]
with \(\mathcal A\) applied in the argument of \(g\).  The right-hand
side has the same scaling form as the left, so the identity can be
iterated for any polynomial in \(\vartheta\), and evaluation at
\(w=1\) replaces the argument by \(y\).
Since \(w=uv\),
\[
\left.
\partial_u^p\partial_v^q F(uv)
\right|_{u=v=1}
=\left.
(\vartheta)\fall{p}(\vartheta)\fall{q}F(w)
\right|_{w=1}.
\]
It follows that
\begin{equation}
D_{pq}^{\alpha,\beta}
=(\mathcal A)\fall{p}(\mathcal A)\fall{q}L(y).
\label{eq:D-operator}
\end{equation}

Define
\[
f(s)=\frac{s^2}{1+s^2}
\]
and the bi-moments
\begin{equation}
B_{pq}
=\int_0^\infty
s^p f(s)^q
\e^{-ys}s^{\alpha-1/2}(1+s^2)^{-\beta-1/2}\dd s.
\label{eq:B-moments}
\end{equation}
Each \(w\)-derivative of the integrand in \eqref{eq:Phi} multiplies
it by a factor that is bounded uniformly for \(w\) in compact subsets
of \((0,\infty)\), and each \(y\)-derivative multiplies it by \(-s\);
the differentiated integrands are therefore dominated, locally
uniformly in \((w,y)\), by integrable functions of \(s\).  This
justifies every differentiation under the integral sign in this
proof.  Since \((\vartheta)\fall{q}=w^q\partial_w^q\), direct
differentiation of \eqref{eq:Phi} with respect to \(w\) gives
\begin{equation}
(\mathcal A)\fall{q}L(y)
=(-1)^q(b)_qB_{0q},
\label{eq:A-on-L}
\end{equation}
where \((b)_q\) is the rising Pochhammer symbol.  On the other hand,
\begin{equation}
(\mathcal A)\fall{p}
=\sum_{i=0}^p c_{pi}(y)\partial_y^i,
\qquad
c_{pp}(y)=\left(-\frac y2\right)^p.
\label{eq:A-expansion}
\end{equation}
Since \(\partial_y^iB_{0q}=(-1)^iB_{iq}\),
\eqref{eq:D-operator}--\eqref{eq:A-expansion} give a factorization
\begin{equation}
D=RB\Lambda,
\label{eq:triangular-factorization}
\end{equation}
because, entrywise,
\begin{equation}
D_{pq}^{\alpha,\beta}
=(-1)^q(b)_q\sum_{i=0}^p
(-1)^ic_{pi}(y)B_{iq}.
\label{eq:D-entry-factorization}
\end{equation}
Here and below the factorization is for the \(k\times k\) truncations
of \(D\) and \(B\), where \(R\) is
lower triangular and \(\Lambda\) is diagonal, with entries
\[
R_{pi}=
\begin{cases}
(-1)^i c_{pi}(y),&0\leq i\leq p,\\
0,&i>p,
\end{cases}
\qquad
\Lambda_{qq}=(-1)^q(b)_q.
\]
Since \(b=\beta+\frac12>0\), every factor \((b)_q\) is strictly
positive.  In particular, the only signs on the diagonal of
\(\Lambda\) are the displayed factors \((-1)^q\).
Also,
\[
R_{pp}=\left(\frac y2\right)^p>0.
\]

It remains to determine the sign of \(\det B\).  Andr\'eief's
integration identity \cite{Andreief1886,Forrester2019} applies because
\(\alpha\geq0\) gives integrability at the origin and \(y>0\) gives
exponential decay at infinity, so every moment in
\eqref{eq:B-moments} is finite.  It gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\det[B_{pq}]_{p,q=0}^{k-1}
=\frac1{k!}\int_{(0,\infty)^k}
&\det[s_j^p]_{p=0,\ldots,k-1}^{j=1,\ldots,k}\\
{}\times&
\det[f(s_j)^q]_{q=0,\ldots,k-1}^{j=1,\ldots,k}
\prod_{j=1}^k\dd\mu(s_j),
\end{split}
\label{eq:andreief}
\end{equation}
where
\[
\dd\mu(s)
=\e^{-ys}s^{\alpha-1/2}
(1+s^2)^{-\beta-1/2}\dd s.
\]
The two determinants in \eqref{eq:andreief} are Vandermonde
determinants.  Because both \(s\) and
\(f(s)=s^2/(1+s^2)\) are strictly increasing on \((0,\infty)\),
their product is nonnegative and is strictly positive whenever the
\(s_j\)'s are distinct.  Moreover, \(\mu\) has a strictly positive
density on \((0,\infty)\), while the diagonals \(s_i=s_j\) have
\(\mu^{\otimes k}\)-measure zero.  Hence \(\det B>0\).

The determinant of \(R\) is positive, whereas
\[
\sgn\det\Lambda=(-1)^{0+1+\cdots+(k-1)}
=(-1)^{k(k-1)/2}.
\]
The factorization \eqref{eq:triangular-factorization} proves
\eqref{eq:D-sign}.
\end{proof}

\section{The four positive kernels}\label{sec:blocks}

We first turn the Bessel initial function into a positive integral.
For \(x>0\), the connection formula
\[
J_{-1/4}(x)
=\frac{J_{1/4}(x)-Y_{1/4}(x)}{\sqrt2}
\]
reduces \eqref{eq:h-bessel} to
\begin{equation}
h(a)=\frac{\pi^2a}{8}
\left[
J_{1/4}\left(\frac{a^2}{4}\right)^2
+Y_{1/4}\left(\frac{a^2}{4}\right)^2
\right].
\label{eq:h-amplitude}
\end{equation}
The Dixon--Ferrar formula \cite{DixonFerrar1933} (see also
\cite[entry~6.518]{GradshteynRyzhik2015}) states that, for \(x>0\)
and \(|\operatorname{Re}\nu|<\frac12\),
\begin{equation}
J_\nu(x)^2+Y_\nu(x)^2
=\frac{8\cos(\pi\nu)}{\pi^2}
\int_0^\infty K_{2\nu}(2x\sinh t)\dd t.
\label{eq:dixon-ferrar}
\end{equation}
Using
\[
K_{1/2}(u)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2u}}\e^{-u}
\]
in \eqref{eq:h-amplitude}, and then setting \(s=\sinh t\), gives
\begin{equation}
h(a)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int_0^\infty
\frac{\e^{-a^2s/2}}{\sqrt{s(1+s^2)}}\dd s.
\label{eq:h-positive}
\end{equation}
In particular, differentiation under this positive integral gives
\begin{equation}
h(a)>0,
\qquad
h'(a)=-a\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int_0^\infty
\frac{\e^{-a^2s/2}\sqrt{s}}{\sqrt{1+s^2}}\dd s<0.
\label{eq:h-first-signs}
\end{equation}
Thus both denominators in the prescribed initial values
\eqref{eq:initial-values} are nonzero for \(a>0\).  Positivity of
\(h''\), and hence positivity of \(v_1\), will follow from the third
kernel in Lemma~\ref{lem:four-kernels}.

Set
\[
y=\frac{a^2}{2},
\qquad
C=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}.
\]
We now introduce an angular source.  For \(u,v>0\), define the radial
generating function
\begin{equation}
\mathcal Z(a;u,v)
=\pi\int_0^\infty
\e^{-at}J_0\left(\frac{t^2\sqrt{uv}}2\right)\dd t.
\label{eq:source}
\end{equation}
This is the angularly integrated two-source expression.  It is not
being defined as a two-dimensional integral away from \(u=v\); such an
integral need not be absolutely convergent.  For each fixed \(R\),
however, termwise angular integration is legitimate and gives
\begin{equation}
\int_0^{2\pi}
\exp\left(
\frac{\ii R^4}{4}
(u\e^{4\ii\theta}+v\e^{-4\ii\theta})
\right)\dd\theta
=2\pi J_0\left(\frac{R^4\sqrt{uv}}2\right),
\label{eq:angular-source}
\end{equation}
and \(t=R^2\) gives \eqref{eq:source}.  On compact subsets of
\((0,\infty)^2\), every \(u,v\)-derivative of the Bessel integrand is
bounded by \(\e^{-at}\) times a polynomial in \(t\): every derivative
of \(J_0\) is a finite linear combination of integer-order Bessel
functions, which are bounded on the real axis, and the chain-rule
factors involving \(u\) and \(v\) are uniformly bounded on such a
compact set.  Thus derivatives may be passed under the \(t\)-integral.
The precise comparison with the planar moments is recorded in
Lemma~\ref{lem:source-moments} below.

\begin{lemma}[source normalization]\label{lem:source-normalization}
For every \(a>0\),
\begin{equation}
\int_{\C}\rho_a(z)\dd A(z)
=\pi\int_0^\infty \e^{-at}J_0\left(\frac{t^2}{2}\right)\dd t
=\mathcal Z(a;1,1)
=h(a),
\label{eq:h-planar}
\end{equation}
where \(h\) is the Bessel combination \eqref{eq:h-bessel}.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Polar coordinates and \eqref{eq:angular-source}, with \(u=v=1\),
give the first two equalities in \eqref{eq:h-planar}.  It remains to
identify the radial Laplace integral with the Bessel combination,
including its normalization.  Put
\[
Z(a)=\pi\int_0^\infty \e^{-at}J_0(t^2/2)\dd t,
\qquad
g(t)=J_0(t^2/2).
\]
The Bessel equation for \(J_0\) gives
\[
g''(t)+\frac1t g'(t)+t^2g(t)=0,
\qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad
-t^3g(t)=(tg'(t))'.
\]
At the origin, the power series for \(J_0\) gives
\[
g(t)=1+O(t^4),\qquad g'(t)=O(t^3),
\]
so in particular \(tg'(t)\to0\).  At infinity, \(g\) and \(g'\) have at
most polynomial growth, while \(\e^{-at}\) gives exponential decay.
These estimates justify the following differentiations and ensure that
all boundary terms in the two integrations by parts vanish.  More
explicitly,
\[
\int_0^\infty \e^{-at}(tg'(t))'\dd t
=a\int_0^\infty \e^{-at}tg'(t)\dd t
=a\int_0^\infty \e^{-at}(at-1)g(t)\dd t.
\]
We therefore obtain
\[
\begin{split}
Z'''(a)+a^2Z'(a)+aZ(a)
={}&\pi\int_0^\infty \e^{-at}
\bigl(-t^3-a^2t+a\bigr)g(t)\dd t\\
={}&0.
\end{split}
\]
Moreover, after setting \(u=at\), boundedness of \(J_0\) on the real
axis and dominated convergence yield
\begin{equation}
\lim_{a\to\infty}aZ(a)
=\pi\int_0^\infty\e^{-u}\dd u=\pi.
\label{eq:Z-normalization}
\end{equation}

These two properties determine \(Z\) uniquely within the
three-dimensional solution space: for any solution \(H\), the
existence of a finite limit of \(aH(a)\) eliminates the two
independent oscillatory combinations, and
the value of that limit fixes the remaining coefficient.  To verify
this explicitly, let \(Q\) be a product of two solutions of the Bessel
equation of order \(\nu\).  Its symmetric-square equation is
\[
x^2Q'''+3xQ''
 +(4x^2+1-4\nu^2)Q'+4xQ=0.
\]
With \(x=a^2/4\) and \(\nu=1/4\), direct substitution shows that
\(H(a)=aQ(a^2/4)\) satisfies
\[
H'''+a^2H'+aH=0
\]
and hence that the solution space of this equation is spanned by
\[
aJ_\nu(x)^2,\qquad
aJ_\nu(x)Y_\nu(x),\qquad
aY_\nu(x)^2.
\]
These three functions are linearly independent because, on any
interval avoiding the zeros of \(Y_\nu\), a linear relation among them
would be a quadratic polynomial relation in the function
\(J_\nu/Y_\nu\); the latter is nonconstant there, since the Wronskian
identity \cite[equation~(10.5.2)]{NISTHandbook2010} gives
\((J_\nu/Y_\nu)'=-2/(\pi xY_\nu^2)\neq0\).
Since the equation has order three, they form a basis of its solution
space.  With \(\chi=x-\nu\pi/2-\pi/4\), the standard large-\(x\)
Bessel asymptotics
\cite[\S10.17, equations~(10.17.3)--(10.17.4)]{NISTHandbook2010}
give
\[
\begin{split}
a\bigl[aJ_\nu(x)^2\bigr]
&=\frac4\pi\bigl(1+\cos(2\chi)\bigr)+O(a^{-2}),\\
a\bigl[aJ_\nu(x)Y_\nu(x)\bigr]
&=\frac4\pi\sin(2\chi)+O(a^{-2}),\\
a\bigl[aY_\nu(x)^2\bigr]
&=\frac4\pi\bigl(1-\cos(2\chi)\bigr)+O(a^{-2}).
\end{split}
\]
Write a general solution as
\[
H=c_1aJ_\nu(x)^2+c_2aJ_\nu(x)Y_\nu(x)+c_3aY_\nu(x)^2.
\]
Since \(2\chi=a^2/2-3\pi/4\) is continuous, strictly increasing, and
unbounded, there are sequences tending to infinity on which its value
modulo \(2\pi\) is respectively \(0,\pi/2,\pi,3\pi/2\).  Comparing the
limit of \(aH(a)\) along these four sequences shows that
\(c_1-c_3=0\) and \(c_2=0\).  The condition
\(\lim_{a\to\infty}aH(a)=\pi\) then gives
\(c_1=c_3=\pi^2/8\).  Thus the unique solution with the prescribed
limit is
\[
H(a)=\frac{\pi^2a}{8}
\left[
J_{1/4}\left(\frac{a^2}{4}\right)^2
+Y_{1/4}\left(\frac{a^2}{4}\right)^2
\right].
\]
This is \(h(a)\) by \eqref{eq:h-amplitude}, proving the final equality
in \eqref{eq:h-planar}.  The radial representation and its \(d=4\)
Bessel evaluation also appear in
\cite[pp.~10--11]{FelderHoppe2025}.
\end{proof}

Put \(w=uv\).  The change of variables \(t=w^{-1/4}x\) in
\eqref{eq:source}, followed by \eqref{eq:h-positive}, now gives
\[
\mathcal Z(a;u,v)
=w^{-1/4}h(aw^{-1/4}),
\]
or, more explicitly,
\[
\mathcal Z(a;u,v)
=Cw^{-1/4}\int_0^\infty
\frac{\e^{-yw^{-1/2}t}}{\sqrt{t(1+t^2)}}\dd t.
\]
The further substitution \(t=\sqrt w\,s\) therefore gives
\begin{equation}
\mathcal Z(a;u,v)
=C\int_0^\infty
\frac{\e^{-ys}}{\sqrt{s(1+uvs^2)}}\dd s
=C\Phi_{0,0}(uv;y).
\label{eq:source-positive}
\end{equation}
For \(r=0,1,2,3\), put
\[
\mathcal Z_r=(-\partial_a)^r\mathcal Z.
\]

\begin{lemma}[source derivatives and planar moments]
\label{lem:source-moments}
For \(r\in\{0,1,2,3\}\) and \(p,q\geq0\),
\begin{equation}
\left.
\partial_u^p\partial_v^q\mathcal Z_r(a;u,v)
\right|_{u=v=1}
=\left(\frac{\ii}{4}\right)^{p+q}
\int_{\C}z^{r+4p}\bar z^{r+4q}\rho_a(z)\dd A(z).
\label{eq:source-moments}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix a compact rectangle \(Q\subset(0,\infty)^2\) containing
\((1,1)\).  Repeated differentiation of the Bessel integrand in
\eqref{eq:source}, using the standard derivative recurrences for
\(J_\nu\), gives, for fixed \(p,q,r\), a bound of the form
\[
\left|
\partial_u^p\partial_v^q(-\partial_a)^r
\left[
\e^{-at}J_0\left(\frac{t^2\sqrt{uv}}2\right)
\right]\right|
\leq C_{Q,p,q,r}\e^{-at}(1+t)^{2p+2q+r}.
\]
Thus dominated convergence permits all the indicated derivatives to
pass through the \(t\)-integral.

For \(R>0\), define the truncated radial source
\[
\mathcal Z^{[R]}(a;u,v)
=\pi\int_0^{R^2}
\e^{-at}J_0\left(\frac{t^2\sqrt{uv}}2\right)\dd t,
\qquad
\mathcal Z_r^{[R]}=(-\partial_a)^r\mathcal Z^{[R]}.
\]
For real \(u,v>0\), also put
\[
\mathcal I^{[R]}(a;u,v)
=\int_{|z|\leq R}
\exp\left(
-a|z|^2+\frac{\ii}{4}(uz^4+v\bar z^4)
\right)\dd A(z).
\]
Applying \eqref{eq:angular-source} in polar coordinates gives
\(\mathcal I^{[R]}=\mathcal Z^{[R]}\).  All \(u,v\)-derivatives here
are real parameter derivatives, and all differentiations are over the
compact disk.  At \(u=v=1\), each \(u\)-derivative
contributes \(\ii z^4/4\), each \(v\)-derivative contributes
\(\ii\bar z^4/4\), and each \(-\partial_a\) contributes
\(|z|^2=z\bar z\).  Therefore
\[
\left.
\partial_u^p\partial_v^q\mathcal Z_r^{[R]}(a;u,v)
\right|_{u=v=1}
=\left(\frac{\ii}{4}\right)^{p+q}
\int_{|z|\leq R}
z^{r+4p}\bar z^{r+4q}\rho_a(z)\dd A(z).
\]
On the radial side, the preceding integrable bound and dominated
convergence show that the left-hand side converges to the left-hand
side of \eqref{eq:source-moments} as \(R\to\infty\).  On the planar
side, the integrand is bounded in absolute value by
\(|z|^{2r+4p+4q}\e^{-a|z|^2}\), so dominated convergence gives the
full planar moment.  This proves \eqref{eq:source-moments}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:four-kernels}
The four radial source functions are
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{c|c}
r&\mathcal Z_r(a;u,v)\\ \hline
0&C\Phi_{0,0}(uv;y),\\[1mm]
1&aC\Phi_{1,0}(uv;y),\\[1mm]
2&2C\Phi_{1,1}(uv;y),\\[1mm]
3&2aC\Phi_{2,1}(uv;y).
\end{array}
\label{eq:four-kernels}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The first two rows follow by differentiating
\eqref{eq:source-positive}.  For the third row, integration by parts
gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
y\int_0^\infty
\frac{\e^{-ys}s^{3/2}}{\sqrt{1+uvs^2}}\dd s
={}&\frac12\int_0^\infty
\frac{\e^{-ys}s^{1/2}}{\sqrt{1+uvs^2}}\dd s\\
&+\int_0^\infty
\frac{\e^{-ys}s^{1/2}}{(1+uvs^2)^{3/2}}\dd s.
\end{split}
\label{eq:ibp-kernel}
\end{equation}
The boundary terms vanish at \(0\) and \(\infty\).  Since
\[
(-\partial_a)^2
=\partial_y+2y\partial_y^2,
\]
equation \eqref{eq:ibp-kernel} gives
\(\mathcal Z_2=2C\Phi_{1,1}\).  Since
\[
\partial_y\Phi_{1,1}(uv;y)=-\Phi_{2,1}(uv;y),
\qquad -\partial_a=-a\partial_y,
\]
one more differentiation gives
\[
\mathcal Z_3
=-\partial_a\mathcal Z_2
=2aC\Phi_{2,1}(uv;y).
\]
In particular, at \(u=v=1\),
\[
h''(a)=\mathcal Z_2(a;1,1)
=2C\Phi_{1,1}(1;y)>0.
\]
\end{proof}

For \(k\geq1\), set
\begin{equation}
\Delta_k^{(r)}
=\det[G_{pq}^{(r)}]_{p,q=0}^{k-1},
\qquad
\Delta_0^{(r)}=1.
\label{eq:Delta}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:block-signs}
For every \(r\in\{0,1,2,3\}\) and \(k\geq1\),
\begin{equation}
\sgn\Delta_k^{(r)}
=(-1)^{rk+k(k-1)/2}.
\label{eq:block-sign}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let
\[
D_{pq}^{(r)}
=\left.
\partial_u^p\partial_v^q\mathcal Z_r(a;u,v)
\right|_{u=v=1}.
\]
Every scalar multiplier in \eqref{eq:four-kernels} is positive.
Lemma~\ref{lem:leading-source-signs}, applied to the four parameter pairs
\[
(\alpha,\beta)=(0,0),(1,0),(1,1),(2,1),
\]
therefore gives
\begin{equation}
\sgn\det[D_{pq}^{(r)}]_{p,q=0}^{k-1}
=(-1)^{k(k-1)/2}.
\label{eq:Dr-sign}
\end{equation}

Lemma~\ref{lem:source-moments}, combined with
\eqref{eq:gram-block}, gives
\begin{equation}
G_{pq}^{(r)}
=(-1)^{r+q}\left(\frac4\ii\right)^{p+q}
D_{pq}^{(r)}.
\label{eq:G-D}
\end{equation}
Let \(S_k=k(k-1)/2\).  Factoring the row and column phases in
\eqref{eq:G-D} yields
\begin{equation}
\Delta_k^{(r)}
=(-1)^{rk}16^{S_k}
\det[D_{pq}^{(r)}]_{p,q=0}^{k-1}.
\label{eq:Delta-D}
\end{equation}
Indeed, taking \((4/\ii)^p\) from row \(p\) and
\((-1)^{r+q}(4/\ii)^q\) from column \(q\) produces
\[
\left(\frac4\ii\right)^{S_k}
(-1)^{rk+S_k}\left(\frac4\ii\right)^{S_k}
=(-1)^{rk}16^{S_k},
\]
because \((4/\ii)^2=-16\).
Now \eqref{eq:block-sign} follows from \eqref{eq:Dr-sign}.
For example, the first two cases give the useful checks
\[
\sgn\Delta_1^{(r)}=(-1)^r,
\qquad
\sgn\Delta_2^{(r)}=-1.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[positive multiple-integral formula]
\label{cor:block-integrals}
For \(r=0,1,2,3\), define
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
r&A_r&\alpha_r&\beta_r\\ \hline
0&C&0&0\\
1&aC&1&0\\
2&2C&1&1\\
3&2aC&2&1
\end{array}
\qquad
\left(C=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}},\quad y=\frac{a^2}{2}\right).
\label{eq:block-parameters}
\end{equation}
For \(k\geq1\), if \(S_k=k(k-1)/2\), then
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
(-1)^{rk+S_k}\Delta_k^{(r)}
={}&\frac{A_r^k(4a^2)^{S_k}}{k!}
\prod_{q=0}^{k-1}\left(\beta_r+\frac12\right)_q\\
&{}\times
\int_{(0,\infty)^k}
\prod_{j=1}^k
\frac{\e^{-ys_j}s_j^{\alpha_r-1/2}}
{(1+s_j^2)^{\beta_r+k-1/2}}\\
&\hspace{27mm}\times
\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}
(s_j-s_i)^2(s_i+s_j)
\prod_{j=1}^k\dd s_j .
\end{split}
\label{eq:block-multiple-integral}
\end{equation}
In particular, the right-hand side is strictly positive.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For the parameters in \eqref{eq:block-parameters},
\eqref{eq:four-kernels} says that
\(D^{(r)}=A_rD^{\alpha_r,\beta_r}\).  The triangular factorization
\eqref{eq:triangular-factorization}, with
\(b=\beta_r+\frac12\), gives
\[
\det D^{\alpha_r,\beta_r}
=(-1)^{S_k}\left(\frac y2\right)^{S_k}
\prod_{q=0}^{k-1}\left(\beta_r+\frac12\right)_q
\det B.
\]
Combining this identity with \eqref{eq:Delta-D}, and using
\(16^{S_k}(y/2)^{S_k}=(4a^2)^{S_k}\), fixes the prefactor in
\eqref{eq:block-multiple-integral}.

It remains only to rewrite the Andr\'eief integrand.  For
\(f(s)=s^2/(1+s^2)\),
\[
f(s_j)-f(s_i)
=\frac{(s_j-s_i)(s_j+s_i)}
{(1+s_i^2)(1+s_j^2)}.
\]
Consequently the product of the two Vandermonde determinants in
\eqref{eq:andreief} is
\[
\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}
\frac{(s_j-s_i)^2(s_i+s_j)}
{(1+s_i^2)(1+s_j^2)}.
\]
Each factor \(1+s_j^2\) occurs \(k-1\) times in the denominator.
Substitution into \eqref{eq:andreief} proves
\eqref{eq:block-multiple-integral}; strict positivity follows because
the integrand is positive away from the diagonals \(s_i=s_j\), which
have measure zero, as in the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:leading-source-signs}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[quartic Gram nondegeneracy]
\label{cor:positive-norms}
For every \(N\geq0\), the leading Gram matrix
\(\big[(z^p,z^q)_a\big]_{p,q=0}^{N}\) is nondegenerate, and the signed
norms in \eqref{eq:signed-norm} satisfy
\[
\fh_n>0\qquad(n\geq0).
\]
In particular, the \(a>0\) part of
\cite[Conjecture~3.6]{FelderHoppe2025} holds for \(d=4\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The block sign formula shows first that every
\(\Delta_k^{(r)}\) is nonzero.  After simultaneously permuting rows and
columns to group monomials by their residue modulo \(4\), every finite
leading Gram matrix is block diagonal, and its blocks are leading
matrices of the four types in \eqref{eq:Delta}.  More precisely, the
block of residue \(r\) has size
\[
k_r(N)=
\begin{cases}
\left\lfloor (N-r)/4\right\rfloor+1,&r\leq N,\\
0,&r>N.
\end{cases}
\]
It is therefore
nondegenerate, and all the monic orthogonal polynomials exist.  Let
\(n=r+4j\).  The orthogonality conditions within \(\mathcal P_r\)
determine the coefficients of \(P_n\) by Cramer's rule, and pairing
\(P_n\) with \(z^{r+4j}\) gives the classical minor ratio
\begin{equation}
(P_n,P_n)_a
=\frac{\Delta_{j+1}^{(r)}}{\Delta_j^{(r)}},
\label{eq:norm-delta-ratio}
\end{equation}
Indeed, replace the last vector \(z^{r+4j}\) in the ordered monomial
basis by \(P_n=z^{r+4j}+\cdots\).  The change-of-basis matrix is
unitriangular, and orthogonality makes the transformed Gram matrix
\[
\operatorname{diag}\left(
[G_{pq}^{(r)}]_{p,q=0}^{j-1},(P_n,P_n)_a
\right).
\]
Its determinant is unchanged, which gives
\(\Delta_{j+1}^{(r)}=\Delta_j^{(r)}(P_n,P_n)_a\); for \(j=0\), this
uses the convention \(\Delta_0^{(r)}=1\).  This is the Schur-complement
identity and requires only the nonvanishing of the leading minors, not
positive definiteness; compare \cite[Section~2.2]{Szego1975}.
Taking the quotient of the signs in \eqref{eq:block-sign} gives
\[
\sgn(P_n,P_n)_a=(-1)^{r+j}.
\]
But \eqref{eq:rho-sign} gives
\(\varrho(r+4j)=(-1)^{r+j}\).  Hence
\(\fh_n=\varrho(n)(P_n,P_n)_a>0\).
By the identification at the end of Section~\ref{sec:gram}, the
nondegeneracy assertion is exactly the \(d=4\), \(a>0\) case of
\cite[Conjecture~3.6]{FelderHoppe2025}.
\end{proof}

The multiple-integral formula also gives the norms and recurrence
coefficients directly as determinant ratios.  Namely, if \(n=r+4j\),
then
\begin{equation}
\fh_n=\frac{|\Delta_{j+1}^{(r)}|}{|\Delta_j^{(r)}|},
\qquad
v_n=\frac1a\frac{\fh_{n+1}}{\fh_n},
\label{eq:integral-ratios}
\end{equation}
where each determinant on the right is given explicitly by
\eqref{eq:block-multiple-integral}.  Since the integrand in
\eqref{eq:block-multiple-integral} is positive, this evaluation is
free of cancellation, in contrast with a direct computation of the
oscillatory moment determinants.

\section{Identification with the quartic recurrence}
\label{sec:completion}

It remains to verify that the positive norm ratios give precisely the
sequence in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.  Define
\begin{equation}
V_n=\frac{\fh_{n+1}}{\fh_n},
\qquad
v_n=\frac{V_n}{a}.
\label{eq:ratios}
\end{equation}
For the recurrence formulas below, also set \(V_j=0\) for \(j<0\), in
agreement with the convention \(v_j=0\) in
\eqref{eq:quartic-recurrence}.
Corollary~\ref{cor:positive-norms} already gives
\begin{equation}
v_n>0\qquad(n\geq0).
\label{eq:ratio-positive}
\end{equation}

For clarity, we include the recurrence calculation from the quartic
Gram form; it is the specialization of
\cite[Lemma~3.4 and equations~(3.1)--(3.3)]{FelderHoppe2025}.  If \(g\)
has \(C_4\)-weight \(\ell\), the definition of the twist gives
\begin{equation}
\sigma(zg)=-\tau(\ell)z\,\sigma g.
\label{eq:sigma-multiplication}
\end{equation}
Indeed, for \(\ell=0,1,2\) this follows directly by comparing the
twist factors in weights \(\ell\) and \(\ell+1\); for \(\ell=3\), the
weight returns to \(0\), and \(\eta^4=-1\) supplies the exceptional
sign \(\tau(3)=-1\).

We use the Wirtinger derivative
\(\partial_z=\frac12(\partial_x-\ii\partial_y)\).  Then
\(\partial_z\overline{\sigma g(z)}=0\) and
\[
\partial_z\rho_a(z)=(-a\bar z+\ii z^3)\rho_a(z).
\]
Differentiating \eqref{eq:density} and integrating by parts in
\eqref{eq:pairing}, with the boundary term removed by the Gaussian
decay, therefore give
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\big((\partial_z+\ii z^3)f,g\big)_a
&=\int_{\C}(\partial_zf+\ii z^3f)
\overline{\sigma g}\,\rho_a\dd A\\
&=a\int_{\C}f\bar z\,\overline{\sigma g}\,\rho_a\dd A\\
&=-\tau(\ell)a(f,zg)_a,
\end{split}
\label{eq:ibp-pairing}
\end{equation}
where the last equality uses \eqref{eq:sigma-multiplication}.  We spell
out the bandedness consequence, since
weight considerations alone do not exclude more distant lower terms.
Write \(L=\partial_z+\ii z^3\).  If \(j\leq n-7\) has the same weight
as \(zP_n\), then \(\deg LP_j\leq j+3<n\), and
\[
(LP_j,P_n)_a=-\tau(n)a(P_j,zP_n)_a=0.
\]
Hermitian symmetry therefore gives \((zP_n,P_j)_a=0\).  Similarly, if
\(j\leq n-5\) has the same weight as \(LP_n\), then
\[
(LP_n,P_j)_a=-\tau(j)a(P_n,zP_j)_a=0,
\]
because \(\deg(zP_j)<n\).  Weight, degree, and orthogonality now give
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
zP_n
&=P_{n+1}+\frac{\ii}{a}\frac{\fh_n}{\fh_{n-3}}P_{n-3},\\
(\partial_z+\ii z^3)P_n
&=\ii P_{n+3}+a\frac{\fh_n}{\fh_{n-1}}P_{n-1},
\end{split}
\label{eq:polynomial-recurrences}
\end{equation}
where terms with negative indices are omitted.  Pairing the first
expansion with \(P_{n-3}\), and using
\eqref{eq:ibp-pairing} with \(f=P_{n-3}\), \(g=P_n\), determines its
lower coefficient.  Pairing the second expansion with \(P_{n-1}\)
determines the other one.  In both calculations one uses
\[
\varrho(n)=\tau(n)\varrho(n-3),
\qquad
\varrho(n)=-\tau(n-1)\varrho(n-1).
\]
For completeness, if \(c_n\) is the coefficient of \(P_{n-3}\) in
\(zP_n\), then
\[
\begin{split}
\ii\varrho(n)\fh_n
&=(LP_{n-3},P_n)_a
=-\tau(n)a(P_{n-3},zP_n)_a,\\
c_n
&=\frac{(zP_n,P_{n-3})_a}{\varrho(n-3)\fh_{n-3}}
=\frac{\ii}{a}\frac{\fh_n}{\fh_{n-3}},
\end{split}
\]
where Hermitian symmetry and
\(\varrho(n)=\tau(n)\varrho(n-3)\) are used in the second line.
Similarly, if \(d_n\) is the coefficient of \(P_{n-1}\) in \(LP_n\),
then
\[
\begin{split}
d_n
&=\frac{(LP_n,P_{n-1})_a}{\varrho(n-1)\fh_{n-1}}\\
&=-\tau(n-1)a
\frac{(P_n,zP_{n-1})_a}{\varrho(n-1)\fh_{n-1}}
=a\frac{\fh_n}{\fh_{n-1}},
\end{split}
\]
because the leading term of \(zP_{n-1}\) is \(P_n\) and
\(\varrho(n)=-\tau(n-1)\varrho(n-1)\).  These computations also make
the phase cancellation in \eqref{eq:polynomial-recurrences} explicit.

The coefficient of \(P_{n-1}\) in \(z^3P_n\) receives three
contributions: the downward step in the first formula of
\eqref{eq:polynomial-recurrences} may occur after zero, one, or two
upward steps.  Hence that coefficient is
\[
\frac{\ii}{a}V_{n-1}
\left(
V_{n-2}V_{n-3}
+V_nV_{n-2}
+V_{n+1}V_n
\right).
\]
Subtracting \(\ii z^3P_n\) from the second formula in
\eqref{eq:polynomial-recurrences}, and using that the coefficient of
\(P_{n-1}\) in \(\partial_zP_n\) is \(n\) (the basis is monic and
graded, so this coefficient is the leading coefficient of
\(\partial_zP_n\)), gives, after putting
\(m=n-1\),
\begin{equation}
V_m\left[
1+\frac1{a^2}
\left(
V_{m-1}V_{m-2}
+V_{m+1}V_{m-1}
+V_{m+2}V_{m+1}
\right)
\right]
=\frac{m+1}{a}.
\label{eq:V-recurrence}
\end{equation}
Here \(n\geq1\), hence \(m\geq0\); the convention \(V_j=0\) for
\(j<0\) incorporates the omitted negative-index terms when \(m=0,1\).
For example, at \(m=0\), equation \eqref{eq:V-recurrence} reads
\[
V_0\left(1+\frac{V_2V_1}{a^2}\right)=\frac1a,
\]
and after \(V_j=av_j\) this is
\[
v_0(1+v_2v_1)=a^{-2},
\]
which is exactly the \(n=0\) case of
\eqref{eq:quartic-recurrence}.
With \(V_m=av_m\), equation \eqref{eq:V-recurrence} is
\eqref{eq:quartic-recurrence}; before that substitution, it is the
case \(d=4\) of \eqref{eq:FH-recurrence}.

Finally, \(P_0=1\), \(P_1=z\), and \(P_2=z^2\).  From
\eqref{eq:h-planar}, differentiation under the absolutely convergent
planar integral gives
\[
h'(a)=-\int_{\C}|z|^2\rho_a(z)\dd A(z),
\qquad
h''(a)=\int_{\C}|z|^4\rho_a(z)\dd A(z).
\]
The twist signs \eqref{eq:twist-sign} and
\(\varrho(0)=-\varrho(1)=\varrho(2)=1\) consequently give
\[
\fh_0=h,\qquad \fh_1=-h',\qquad \fh_2=h''.
\]
Therefore \eqref{eq:ratios} gives exactly
\eqref{eq:initial-values}.  Conversely, solving
\eqref{eq:quartic-recurrence} for its newest term gives
\begin{equation}
v_{m+2}
=\frac{(m+1)a^{-2}/v_m-1-v_{m-1}v_{m-2}
-v_{m+1}v_{m-1}}{v_{m+1}},
\qquad m\geq0.
\label{eq:forward-recurrence}
\end{equation}
Thus \(v_{m+2}\) is uniquely determined by the
preceding terms whenever \(v_mv_{m+1}\neq0\).  The norm-ratio sequence
\eqref{eq:ratios} is positive, so \eqref{eq:forward-recurrence} applies
at every step and constructs a global extension of the prescribed
initial data.  If \((\widetilde v_n)\) is any other extension, then
\(\widetilde v_0=v_0\) and \(\widetilde v_1=v_1\).  Assuming
\(\widetilde v_j=v_j\) through \(j=m+1\), the positivity of
\(v_mv_{m+1}\) allows the equation at index \(m\) to be solved for its
newest term, forcing \(\widetilde v_{m+2}=v_{m+2}\).  Induction proves
uniqueness.  Together with \eqref{eq:ratio-positive}, this proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.

\begin{remark}
The uniqueness in Theorem~\ref{thm:main} concerns the sequence with the
prescribed initial data.  The argument does not claim a classification
of positive solutions with other initial data, a closed evaluation of
the Gram determinants, asymptotic formulas for \(v_n\), or a result at
the endpoint \(a=0\).
\end{remark}
